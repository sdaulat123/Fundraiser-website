Undiagnosed Ghost: Treating the Symptom While Missing the Source
Dr. Gregory Hamilton
There’s a hard truth in clinical work—especially in IOP settings: we are often not treating the person… we are treating the presentation. The anxiety, the substance use, the failed relationships, the job instability. But underneath it all? There’s often an “undiagnosed ghost”—a personality structure driving the chaos.
The Numbers Don’t Lie — But They Don’t Tell the Whole Story
Roughly 9.1% of U.S. adults meet criteria for a diagnosable personality disorder?
National Institute of Mental Health
Globally, estimates hover around 7.8%–10% of the population 
PubMed +1
Yet here’s the kicker:
Only ~39% receive any treatment 
National Institute of Mental Health
Meaning the majority are walking around undiagnosed or misdiagnosed
Now push deeper:
Up to 37% of people show multiple personality disorder traits—enough to suggest clinical significance, even without diagnosis 
Springer (2015)
84.5% of those with personality disorders have another mental disorder (depression, anxiety, substance use, etc.) 
National Institute of Mental Health
 Translation: what we diagnose (depression, addiction, anxiety) is often just the surface expression.
The Functional Fallout (What You See in Real Life)
1. Relationship Instability
Personality disorders—especially Cluster B—are defined by unstable, intense, or chaotic relationships �
MSD Manuals
Idealization → devaluation cycles
Fear of abandonment → controlling or reactive behaviors
Emotional volatility → relational burnout
Clinical reality:
Multiple failed relationships aren’t “bad luck.” They are often repeating relational templates.
2. Employment Instability
Research consistently shows significant functional impairment and occupational difficulty in individuals with personality disorders
(PubMed)
Conflict with authority
Inconsistent performance
Impulsivity or emotional reactivity
Clinical reality:
It’s not “they can’t keep a job”—it’s their internal structure disrupts external stability.
3. Isolation & Loneliness
Loneliness is highly prevalent in individuals with personality pathology cited 
SpringerLink
Push-pull dynamics drive people away
Mistrust or avoidance limits connection
Shame reinforces withdrawal
Clinical reality:
“I don’t need anyone” often translates to “I’ve been unable to sustain anyone.”
The Core Problem: Lack of Insight (The Ghost Effect)
Thoughts I have so far are, 
“You point out a problem to the person, you become the problem.”
This is clinically accurate.
Personality disorders are ego-syntonic:
The individual experiences their behavior as normal
The problem is perceived as externalized
So you hear:
“I don’t have a problem—you’re the problem”
“Every relationship fails because people are fake”
“Jobs don’t work out because management is incompetent”
👉 This is not resistance.
👉 This is structural lack of insight.
IOP Reality: Treating the Ghost
In intensive outpatient settings:
Clients present with:
Substance use
Depression
Legal issues
But underneath:
Identity disturbance
Emotional dysregulation
Maladaptive relational patterns
So what happens?
We treat:
The drinking
The anxiety
The crisis
…but ignore:
The personality architecture driving all three
Clinical Example (Composite Case)
“Marcus” (Composite)
32 years old
6 jobs in 5 years
4 failed long-term relationships
Daily cannabis use
Reports: “People always betray me”
Treatment focus:
Substance use → partial improvement
Depression → recurring
Missed factor:
Underlying paranoid/cluster B traits:
Mistrust
Emotional reactivity
Identity instability
👉 Result: symptoms rotate, pattern persists
The Real Clinical Challenge
You said it best:
“Clinicians are trying to treat the ghost instead of the human host.”
Look Closer 
The ghost = symptoms (anxiety, addiction, depression)
The host = personality structure
Most systems:
Bill for symptoms
Stabilize crises
Discharge early
Few systems:
Reconstruct identity
Build insight
Address personality organization
Final Take: The First Step Is Still the Hardest
Everything loops back to one truth:
Insight precedes change
Until the client can say:
“I might be part of the pattern”
…nothing stabilizes long-term.
We are not facing a mental health crisis of diagnosis—we are facing a crisis of recognition.
The ghost isn’t rare. It’s just unnamed.
And until we start treating the host, the ghost will keep showing up—in every relationship, every job, and every relapse.
— Dr. Gregory Hamilton
